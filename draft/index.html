Rounds 14 and 15: kicker and defense

Take them with your last two picks, never sooner. Kickers are close to unpredictable year over year, and a streamed defense costs nothing. Spending a mid-round pick here is the most common way beginners lose value.

You are not playing football. You are playing thirteen coworkers.

This distinction is the whole strategy. You do not need the objectively best team — you need a team that beats these specific people. That is a much easier problem, because casual drafters make the same mistakes every single year.

Why steady beats explosive here, specifically. You play one opponent per week and only need to beat that one score. If you are the better-prepared manager, you are already the favorite — and a favorite wants less randomness, because variance is what lets a worse team beat you. Underdogs want chaos; favorites want boring. Your instinct to prize consistency is the correct play for someone holding an edge.

The five mistakes to harvest. Watch for these and take the value they leave behind:

  1. Drafting a quarterback too early. Quarterbacks score the most raw points, so beginners reach. But you start only one, and the 14th-best is nearly as good as the 5th. Every early quarterback taken pushes a real starter down to you.
  2. Taking a kicker or defense before the last two rounds. Pure gift. Let them.
  3. Homer picks. Someone will take a player from their favorite team two rounds early. Free value for everyone else.
  4. Chasing last season. Casual drafters rank by what happened last year rather than what is projected this year.
  5. Panic during a run. When three backs go in a row, someone always reaches. Being one pick ahead of a run is profit; being three picks ahead is a reach.

Tiers matter more than rankings. The real question at your pick is never "who is best?" — it is "how many players this good will survive until my next turn?" If six players remain in a tier and ten picks happen before you pick again, the tier is gone, so take one now. If twelve remain, wait and take the scarcer position. The tier lines on the board are there for exactly this.

Do not stack your own team. A quarterback and his receiver boom together and bust together. That is great if you need variance and actively bad if you want a steady floor. The board warns you when three of your starters share an NFL team.

The draft is not where most of the edge is. In a casual league, more value moves after the draft than during it. People drop good players after two bad weeks, forget to set lineups, and accept lopsided trades. Checking the waiver wire every Tuesday is probably worth more than anything you do tomorrow.

How the ranking works

Value is not raw points. It is how many points a player scores above the last startable player at his own position in a 14-team league. A quarterback who scores 330 points is worth less than a running back who scores 240, because you can always find another decent quarterback and you cannot find another decent back.

The 2025 season strip shows all 17 weeks. Green is a top-tier week, red is a week that actively cost you the matchup, grey is ordinary. A player with lots of green and no red is the ideal: high total, few disasters.

The dial re-ranks everyone. Slide toward Steady and players who scored close to their average every week rise; slide toward Explosive and raw point totals take over. Underneath it is a mean-variance tradeoff — each player's projected weekly average minus a penalty for their historical week-to-week swing.

Why the dial defaults to 25, not 100

The default is not a guess. We simulated 50,400 seasons at each setting — drafting a team, then playing a full 13-week schedule against thirteen opponents drafting off the consensus market — with every setting facing identical opponents and identical weekly luck so the comparison is apples to apples. Chasing consistency hard is the worst strategy tested — about nine percentage points of playoff odds worse than a light tilt. The reason is worth understanding, because it is the one genuinely counterintuitive thing on this page.

You already own most of the consistency you are trying to buy. You start nine players, and their good and bad weeks largely cancel out. Across a typical starting nine, the average individual player swings about 52% around his own average — but the team total swings only about 18%. Simply adding up nine players removes roughly two thirds of the volatility for free. Paying a premium in projected points to buy steadier individuals is therefore paying twice for something you mostly already have, and the points you give up are gone for good.

The one place this breaks down is correlation. If your players' weeks are linked — several starters from the same NFL team, or a quarterback throwing to your own receiver — they stop cancelling out. A fully correlated lineup would swing about 52% again, nearly three times worse. That is why the board warns you about stacking, and it is where your attention to consistency actually pays.

Why some players show no week-by-week record. The 2025 column only fills in when there is something real to show. A player who missed most of last season through injury — Malik Nabers tore an ACL four games in, Mike Evans broke a collarbone, Terry McLaurin missed seven games — has no meaningful 2025 pattern, so the board falls back to his measured steadiness across 2023–25 and says so. Players marked no fantasy role in 2025 had no meaningful workload last season — some are genuine rookies, but most are veterans who sat behind someone else. The source table only covers about the top fifty players per position, so falling outside it is not the same as being new. Either way nothing was measured, and their reliability figure is a positional average — treat it as a placeholder rather than a reading. Kickers and defenses have no published consistency table at all, so theirs is an outright estimate, which is one more reason not to spend a real pick on either.

Where the numbers come from. Projections are 2026 preseason estimates scored under your exact Yahoo settings (half point per catch, 4-point passing touchdowns, −2 for fumbles). Week-to-week reliability comes from measured 2025 results, regressed toward the positional average because one season is a small sample. Players marked no track record are rookies — their reliability is a positional average, not a measurement, so treat it as a guess.

How much to trust the simulation. We tested this by perturbing the projections by a realistic ±8% and re-running. Even when the simulation showed a 20–28 point margin, the winner changed about half the time. Running more simulations does not help — 40 runs and 1,000 runs give the same answer. The limit is not computing power or sample size, it is that nobody can project a football season accurately. What the simulation genuinely does is separate the handful of sensible picks from the clearly worse ones. Inside that handful, trust your own read.

Would more data help? Barely. We measured which inputs actually move the recommendation: perturbing the projections changed it ~60% of the time, perturbing the consistency ratings ~17%, and perturbing the market prices 0%. Better projections would matter — but preseason projections are roughly ±20% for everyone, which is a limit of the sport, not a gap in this dataset.

What this cannot do. Projections explain maybe a third of what actually happens in a season. Injuries and touchdown luck do the rest. This board makes you well-calibrated on every pick, which wins more often than not across many seasons. It cannot stop a good draft from going wrong in one.
